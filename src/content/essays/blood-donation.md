“Just a small pinch…” I said, pulling the shiny, latex gloves over my hands. He looked
pretty nervous; it was probably his first time, so I gave him a small, tight-lipped smile. After
doing this about 50 times a day, you start to lose some of that caring patience you walk in with. I
placed an apple-shaped stress ball into his right hand.

“Squeeze that for me.”

He promptly ignored this, keeping his arm stubbornly limp. He refused to respond to any
of my attempts at small talk, which I found to be somewhat rude, for a person who was willingly
donating blood to help other people. As I prepped the needle, I studied his features. His eyes
were a grayish-blue, and glossed over, half-lidded- as if he was barely able to stay awake for this.

I thumbed around the soft flesh of his forearm, right above the elbow. His veins were
very small for a man so large, and I had to press in to feel them. His skin was cold, but that was
no surprise- it’s always freezing in the clinic. After finding a suitable vein, I position the needle
before it, just grazing the skin. “Breathe in for me,” I say, although he doesn’t listen. Tough guy.

I watch the needle go in smoothly, like a meat thermometer in a well-cooked chicken. This was
my favorite part, watching it go up the tube. Blood is a lot darker than people expect. It’s not that
bright, tomatoey red they use in movies. It’s darker, and murky, almost a deep wine.

“All good?”

His head slumped forward in a lazy nod. I sighed, and watched the bag fill up, already
reaching 50 ounces. Somebody in the clinic had left something too long in the fridge, the whole
place smelled like rotten eggs. 80 ounces. And there was a leak in the ceiling that dripped down
into a bucket in the corner. Couldn’t be very sanitary for a clinic. 100. Little things like this made
me wonder, a bit bitterly, why I turned out the way I am, here, living like this every day. I looked
over to the bag. 150 ounces. Full to the brim.

“All done,” I said, pulling out the needle gently, “You did very well, thank you.” I
reached for the bag, unhooking it from the stand. I crack open the top and stick a straw through.

“Ugh. O positive.”
